# Plug Budget Leaks Before Routing Models

Do not lead with the full question (allocate compute across models, strategies and attempts to maximise the chance of a correct patch). Lead with a narrower one that your data can carry: **in test-gated, oracle-localized repair with small open-weight models, where does the inference budget leak, and can cheap runtime signals reclaim it without losing solves?** Your own pilot points there. The model-to-model headroom is small (the oracle router solves 50 of 99 against 46 for gpt-oss-120b alone), the 2x Groq price gap is cancelled by the 20B writing 2.3x more tokens, and 58% of spend and 46% of the 20B's spend produce nothing (hopeless tasks, decoding loops). The literature agrees that routing and cascading are crowded, that their headline savings depend on a free, in-distribution quality signal and a large price gap, and that the best recent evidence for adaptive selection at code scale is a negative result: outcome-blind selectors tied a fixed action ([Resample or Reroute v3](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.08665v3)). Four gaps look open, though "not found in a limited search" is all the notes can support: loop or degeneration detection as an allocation signal, false-positive rate by model tier, runtime-conditional model comparison, and a matched-cost refine-versus-resample comparison at repository level. Narrowed variants are publishable at workshop or middle tier (Findings, industry, software-engineering venues); main track needs a sealed post-cutoff set of about 300 tasks, at least two price books and a strict second label. The full question needs roughly 5-6 arms with a 10x-plus price spread, 3 seeds, about $150-450 of API spend plus days of Docker grading, and even then n=300 resolves only about 6 points between unrelated arms and about 3 points between nested policies, so a learned policy must be a gated final stage, not the headline. Everything here rests on research notes read through summarising fetch tools; several 2026 sources are withdrawn, retitled or abstract-only (see the last section).

## Answers up front: narrow the claim, keep the full question as a gated ladder

Four terms recur. An *arm* is one concrete (model, prompt, sampling, apply-tier) combination. *Offline replay* evaluates a policy by looking up stored outcomes for every arm instead of re-running models. A *router* picks a model before generating; a *cascade* tries a cheap model and escalates when a verifier rejects. The *oracle router* picks, per task, the cheapest arm that passes, which no real policy can match. Your assets are real: a typed recovery runtime, Docker grading with a gold-patch control (99/100), a pre-registered protocol with git tags, and per-run costs around a tenth of a cent. Your weak points are equally clear: the model is handed the target file, decoding ran at temperature 0 against vendor guidance, the price gap is 2x, n is 99, and no sealed set exists yet.

### 1. Best research question: where does the budget leak?

**Recommendation: make the paper about spend accounting and failure-aware stop, retry and escalate rules, with verifier strictness as the second leg.** State it as: in test-gated single-file repair with small open-weight models, what share of tokens and dollars goes to attempts that cannot succeed, which cheap observable signals (decoding loop, edit not applied, failure after k attempts, cross-model escalation) reclaim that spend at an unchanged number of solves, and do the answers survive a stricter label and a different price book? The pilot supplies the motivation: on your numbers, 20B failures are rescued by 120B only 12 times out of 61 (about 20%, Wilson 95% interval roughly 12-31%; my arithmetic from 38/46/50 solves), 49 tasks defeat both models, and the cascade buys 4 extra solves over 120B alone for $0.076 more. Do not claim an optimal policy, a model ranking, or Verified-comparable accuracy. Candidates, ranked by an overall judgment of novelty, feasibility and risk:

## Routing is crowded; verifier-aware spend control is open

**What is saturated.** The foundational routing literature is settled in outline: a cascade with a learned acceptance score matches the best single model at lower cost ([FrugalGPT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.05176)), binary routers send much traffic to a weak model with small loss ([Hybrid LLM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.14618), [RouteLLM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.18665)), and everything is bounded by the quality estimator ([Dekoninck et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.10347)). What the field has also learned, repeatedly, is how conditional those savings are. [RouterBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.12031) found predictive routers do not significantly beat a "zero router" that simply mixes models along the cost-quality convex hull, and cascades helped only when judge error stayed below about 0.1. [LLMRouterBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.07206) (400K instances, 33 models, 10 routers) found several recent and commercial routers do not reliably beat the best single model. [Bouchard's escalation study](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.06350) found two-model cascades beat the best single model by 2.1-8.2 points, and a third model improved mean test accuracy in only 17 of 135 comparisons. Hybrid LLM's "cost advantage" is the share of queries sent to the small model, not dollars, and router or judge cost is usually assumed free ([Hybrid LLM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.14618), [RouterBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.12031)). A 2x price gap sits well below the spread these papers need.

**What is contested or negative at code scale.** At repository level the live direction is trajectory-conditioned escalation: [SWE-Router](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.00053) reads a partial agent trajectory and reaches Route-AUC 0.780 (versus 0.627 without the trajectory) on SWE-bench Verified, but with 100 genuinely held-out instances, no confidence intervals and no routed dollar total. [Scrouting](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.04804) solves 159 of 266 SWE-bench Pro tasks against 158 for the best single model, sends 263 of 266 tasks to one cheap fixer, and attributes the gain to its repository handoff rather than routing; its audit puts solve-set containment on Verified at 0.941, meaning one strong model almost contains the others. [TwinRouterBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.18859) reports a trained router at 75 resolved for $0.26 per task against 74 for $0.55 unrouted on a locked 100-instance Django/Astropy list. The most pointed result is [Resample or Reroute v3](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.08665v3): after a weak verifier accepts a response, there is recoverable error (+2.592 points, 95% CI [+1.618, +3.664], MBPP+, one Qwen pair), but four outcome-blind controllers tied a fixed reroute (0.000 points, CI [-0.987, +1.053]). The author's own earlier claims of Pareto dominance (v1) were removed in v3, which is a warning about offline-replay policy claims in general.

**What is established about verifiers.** Coverage (some sample passes) is not accuracy (the selected sample passes). [Large Language Monkeys](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.21787) reports SWE-bench Lite coverage rising from 15.9% to 56% with 250 samples under an assumed-exact checker, and notes voting and reward models plateau without one. Imperfect tests cap that: resampling cannot reduce the false-positive rate, and a weaker model cannot reach a stronger model's accuracy by sampling ([Stroebl et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.17501)); verifier ROC geometry sets best-of-N and rejection-sampling accuracy ([ROC-n-reroll](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.12399)); and repair loops can lower true validity while reported acceptance rises ([VRR-Stop](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.17641)). The error rates are not small on SWE-bench itself: 7.8% of "correct" patches fail the developer test suite and 29.6% behave differently from the gold patch ([PatchDiff](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.15223)), 15.7% of Verified passes were erroneous under augmented tests ([UTBoost](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.09289)), and re-evaluating the top ten Verified agents drops resolve rates 4.2-9.0 points ([Probe to Generate](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.01518)). These audits all use frontier-agent patches.

**Where the notes found nothing, and how far to trust that.** The following are "not found", not "absent": any paper using decoding-loop detection as an explicit routing or abort signal (the loop literature measures prevalence and fixes, and the nearest allocation work is TAAR's restart and EET's experience-based termination); any measurement of false-positive rate by model size or cost tier; any matched-cost comparison of refinement against resampling on repository-level tasks; any paper formulating models x strategies x attempts jointly under a noisy test verifier at repository level (the nearest are [OSCA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.22480), which allocates samples across model and temperature configurations and reports 3x less compute on SWE-bench, and [Adaptive Generate-Rank-Verify](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.17609), which assumes an exact verifier); and any pre-registration culture in code-repair routing. The notes ran about ten searches for the joint formulation, never queried OpenReview, and could not identify one snippet-level "ICLR 2026 bandit test-time-compute allocation" paper, so identify it before claiming novelty. Also read [Risa, arXiv 2608.22191](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.22191) in full: it reports routing-guided test-time scaling on Verified for the gpt-oss family (macro resolve rate 44.9% to 48.2%), the closest match to your models, and the notes saw only the abstract.

## Why each narrowed variant lands where it does

**Fixed model-pair cascade only.** Technically sound and textbook; commercially empty here. The cascade solves 50 for $0.23, against 46 for $0.154 for 120B alone, an extra $0.019 per extra solve, 5.7 times the average cost per solve, and an exact sign test on the 4 extra solves gives p=0.125. A reviewer sees FrugalGPT with a 2x price gap and no cost win. It survives only as a boundary result: when the price ratio roughly equals the token ratio, cascading cannot save money, and the benefit is bounded by complementarity. That is a workshop-sized observation, and it gets stronger if repeated on a second price book where the 20B really is cheaper.

**Failure-type-conditioned escalation.** The idea is attractive because the Argus runtime already types failures, and a published precedent in the same design space ([CodeRescue](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.19338)) chose among reflect, replan and escalate. But that paper is withdrawn and cannot be cited as evidence. The identification problem is the real obstacle: only 12 of 61 20B failures are rescued, so any per-type conditional is estimated from a handful of events. [Resample or Reroute v3](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.08665v3) shows what happens next: a preregistered two-sided support gate failed by a hair (23 and 22 episodes against a minimum of 25) and the experiment stopped. Present this as exploratory analysis inside the main paper, with a fixed-action baseline (always escalate after rejection), not as a result in its own right.

**Verifier-quality-aware allocation.** This is the most publishable narrowing, because the gap is measurable and software-engineering venues have accepted exactly this kind of validity work (PatchDiff at ICSE 2026, UTBoost at ACL 2025, Probe to Generate at ASE 2026). The unmeasured quantity is how the false-positive rate varies with model tier: the notes record a hypothesis that cheaper models write narrower "make the test pass" patches, but no paper measures it. Your design adds value because you can score every pass twice, once with the official harness and once with a stricter label, and show whether the cascade's and cheap arm's rankings survive. Reviewers will attack the strict label (PatchDiff used GPT-4o-mini tests and first-author labeling), the small number of manual audits, and the single oracle-file setting. Expect low absolute false-positive rates; the finding is the difference between tiers, and a null is still informative.

**Loop and early-abort as the contribution.** The pilot gives it a strong hook (21% of 20B runs, 46% of its spend, no output), and the literature has no allocation paper built on it. The attack is also the most dangerous: the loops appeared at temperature 0 against vendor guidance, [Pipis et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.12895v1) call temperature a stopgap rather than a fix, and [Circular Reasoning](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.05693) shows loop rates falling from 92% to 19% across temperatures for one model. Re-baseline at temperature 1.0 first. If loops fall to a few percent, the paper becomes a short lesson in sampling hygiene; if a residual remains, it becomes interesting. Either way include a matched-budget fresh-sample control (an empty-prefix restart at equal tokens, per a snippet-level 2026 preprint on budget and difficulty confounds, [2609.03436](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.03436)), a check that a long 8k-token completion is a true repetition loop and not merely long reasoning, and a direct comparison to [EET](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.05777), which already saves 19-55% on agent runs.

**Apply layer and edit robustness.** This is your most solid result (7 extra solves on 99; with no offsetting losses, p=0.016), it matches independent evidence on tolerance tiers, and it supports a hook no other paper offers: model rankings depend on the recovery layer. The risks are scope and framing. The effect is specific to gpt-oss's edit format quirks, the fuzzy tier rests on one task, and an engineering paper without a transferability test (a Qwen model, a different edit format) reads as a tool report. It fits workshop or software-engineering tool and industry tracks, and works best as a section in the main paper, since it also changes the arm library that any allocation study replays.

**Negative or identification framing.** Resample or Reroute v3 shows the shape: a gated design, honest nulls, claims retreating from v1 to v3. It is also a single-author preprint with a self-authorized test opening and an evaluator that changed after the first sealed computation, so it is not evidence that this framing clears review. The core problem is arithmetic. At n=300, the smallest difference you can reliably detect between unrelated arms is about 6.4 points, and about 3.2 for nested policies (derivation in the next section). A null that cannot exclude effects below those sizes is uninformative, because plausible policy gains here are 2-4 points. Use identification framing as a discipline (pre-registered gates, stated minimum detectable effect, equivalence margins), not as the headline.

## What the full question demands

**Models and price spread.** A routing study needs a spread large enough that routing can matter. Within gpt-oss on Groq the spread is 2x ([Groq](https://console.groq.com/docs/models)), on OpenRouter 4.6-5x. Pairs with at least 10x on both input and output include Haiku 5.5 versus Sonnet 5.5 (20x, but only for prompts up to 100k tokens, above which the ratio drops to 4x), gpt-5.4-nano versus gpt-5.4 (about 12x), and gpt-oss-20b versus a closed flagship (roughly 67-115x) ([Anthropic](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing), [OpenAI](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/pricing), [OpenRouter](https://openrouter.ai/api/v1/models)). Qwen size ladders within a generation mostly run 2-5x on input. Open-weight-only ladders reach a usable spread by going from gpt-oss-20b to a large thinking model. Measure realized dollars per task rather than list ratios, since cheap reasoning models emit more tokens. Prices are a 2026-11-11 snapshot; a promotional rate ends 30 November and a Gemini price rise follows on 1 December, so freeze a dated price book in the repository. Groq's limits (30 requests per minute, 1K per day, 8K tokens per minute, 200K per day on the page's table) make free keys a bottleneck; use a paid tier or OpenRouter.

**Tasks and statistical power.** Tasks, not draws, are the unit of inference. [Miller's formula](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.00640) gives n = (z + z')^2 omega^2 / delta^2 with omega^2 the variance of the per-task difference. From your pilot, unrelated arms disagree on about 16% of tasks (omega^2 about 0.155) and nested policies such as a cascade versus its second stage on about 4% (omega^2 about 0.04); these are my estimates and will move on a new set. The minimum detectable difference at 80% power, alpha 0.05:

| Tasks (n) | Unrelated arms | Nested policies |
|---|---|---|
| 100 | 11.0 pts | 5.6 pts |
| 200 | 7.8 pts | 4.0 pts |
| 300 | 6.4 pts | 3.2 pts |
| 600 | 4.5 pts | 2.3 pts |

Within-task sampling noise at temperature 1 adds variance; three draws per arm recover part of it, but more than 3-5 buys little because repeated draws cannot cut variance by more than two thirds ([Miller](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.00640)). Nested comparisons (cascade versus second stage, stop rule versus no stop rule) are the cheap ones to power, so write the hypotheses as nested comparisons. Because your machine handles about 100-300 tasks, the minimum viable plan is your 99 as discovery plus 200 new sealed, giving 7.8 and 4.0 points; the full plan adds about 100 discovery tasks and a 300-task sealed set. A cost-quality frontier difference needs its own bootstrap from pilot data; no source gave a formula.

**Arm library, grid and replay.** Build a full-feedback table once: every task by every arm by several draws, then evaluate policies by lookup, which sidesteps the selection bias of logged data ([Causal LLM Routing](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.16037)). The caveat that bites here is feedback-conditioned policies: retry-with-test-output and escalate-after-failure depend on earlier attempts, so the library must store conditional chains (attempt 1, feedback, attempt 2), otherwise the replay is not a valid counterfactual, as [Resample or Reroute](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.08665v3) does with its history attempts. Apply-layer arms need no new generation: store the raw model output and re-apply under each tier offline (grading is the cost). Do not build a full cross product. Run model x 3 draws at the vendor-recommended temperature, add reasoning effort on gpt-oss only, and add feedback chains on 2-3 arms; that is roughly 25-35 generations per task. Log provider, model version and date, since hosted models drift and are not deterministic even at temperature 0.

**Baselines.** The expected set from the routing literature, applied to code repair: each single model; best single model at matched cost; oracle (cheapest passing arm per task, with and without abstention); a zero router that mixes models along the cost-quality convex hull ([RouterBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.12031)); a low-dimensional prompt-feature router (logistic regression or kNN, since [kNN is competitive](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.12601) and 300 tasks cannot support a 7B value head); a fixed cascade with the same verifier; best-of-n or resample-with-verifier at the same spend, which [Dong and Shigida](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.08478) suggest is hard to beat; and the fixed-action baseline (always escalate after rejection). Report a matched-budget operating point and one defined AUC (say which: AIQ and Route-AUC normalize differently). Count verifier and failed-attempt cost; [TwinRouterBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.18859) charges a flat penalty for failed trajectories and warns that naive per-step accounting flatters conservative routers.

**Verifier setup.** Two decisions, both missing from most papers. First, separate the in-loop test from the scoring test. If the model iterates against the gold FAIL_TO_PASS test and is scored on that same test, the label is closed-loop and carries no independent information; the notes also flag that the gold test can leak interface names the issue never states. Options for the in-loop signal are the hidden FAIL_TO_PASS result returned as pass/fail plus truncated output, an agent-generated reproduction test, or the repository's existing tests ([CoHarden](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.19843), [TestPrune](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.18270)); state which one your cascade uses. Second, score twice: the official harness (FAIL_TO_PASS plus PASS_TO_PASS) as the primary label and a strict label for secondary analysis, using the full developer test suite for touched modules, augmented tests where released, and a double-annotated manual audit of a sample of passes by tier. Audit identifiers in the test patch that are absent from the issue. Keep the gold-patch control and list excluded instances; treat the harness's run_id caching as a hazard ([SWE-bench README](https://github.com/SWE-bench/SWE-bench)). Your patch-only, no-shell design has one real advantage: the agent-time leakage seen in Verified and Pro (agents reading future commits through git history) cannot occur ([Cursor](https://cursor.com/blog/reward-hacking-coding-benchmarks)).

**Held-out evaluation.** The notes found no pre-registration practice in this literature, so you set the standard. Verified is contaminated (file-path recall of 60-76% from issue text alone, [Illusion](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.12286v3); OpenAI's withdrawal of Verified is known only through secondary sources, e.g. [Pebblous](https://blog.pebblous.ai/blog/swe-bench-verified-retired/en/)). Differential contamination is the specific threat: a model that has memorized a task looks cheap-and-good, an effect that will not transfer. Draw the sealed set from a post-cutoff pool, [SWE-bench-Live](https://github.com/microsoft/SWE-bench-Live) (1,319 initial Python tasks plus 50 verified per month, per-instance Docker images) and [SWE-rebench](https://swe-rebench.com) (time-windowed, with contamination labels), filter to single-file tasks whose gold patch passes, and take the single-file fraction as unknown until you measure it. Choose the sealed IDs now by SHA-256 hash of the instance ID before you tune anything else, commit the frozen policy, evaluator source hash, price book and analysis plan under a tag, and open the set once. Resample or Reroute's weaknesses are the checklist: self-authorized opening, evaluator changed after first computation, post-outcome robustness arms, and a gate threshold with no power basis. Have an advisor or collaborator hold the seal. Verified remains useful as a secondary replication and for continuity with the pilot.

**Compute and dollars.** Your measured per-run costs are $0.0013 (20B+Argus, $0.128 over 99 tasks) and $0.0016 (120B+Argus). The notes' scenario arithmetic puts 5,400 single-file runs at about $39 (no reasoning) to $139 (with reasoning), dominated by the strongest arm (scenario arithmetic in the notes, using assumed token counts and list prices such as those on [OpenRouter](https://openrouter.ai/api/v1/models)). Scaling to roughly 12-17K generations for 500 tasks, with one strong closed arm and one large open thinking arm, I estimate $100-300 for generation and $150-450 with a 2x contingency; these are assumptions to be replaced with pilot token counts. Dollars are not the binding constraint. Docker grading is: at an assumed minute per evaluation, 8-11K unique applied patches is about 6-8 days of continuous grading, or about 3 days for the minimum viable plan. Measure evaluation time on the pilot, deduplicate by (instance, patch hash), skip patches that fail to apply, and grade all of a task's patches while its image is pulled, then delete the image. The recommended machine for the harness is 16 GB RAM, 8 cores and 120 GB free disk ([SWE-bench README](https://github.com/SWE-bench/SWE-bench)).

**Main risks.** In order of danger: identification failure (the policy gate fails and you have only descriptive results); the temperature-0 and oracle-file reviewer attacks; contamination on any Verified-derived claim; a price gap that makes every cost conclusion provider-specific; the strict label being weaker than the label it audits; replay invalid for feedback-conditioned policies; provider drift and rate limits; forking paths from a large grid (limit pre-specified comparisons to about five and correct for them); and scooping, since 2026 shows heavy activity (SWE-Router, TwinRouterBench, Scrouting, EET, Risa) and your sources include items posted in the last six months.

## Conclusion

Three things changed against the researcher's starting framing. First, the economics are the finding: when a model's price is half its competitor's but its output is 2.3 times longer, there is nothing to arbitrage, and any cascade's value is bounded by complementarity, which here is four solves. Second, the 13-point gap between the two models shrinks to 3-8 points once the 20B gets runtime repairs, so "which model is better" is partly a statement about the recovery layer, and that is a result others cannot reproduce without a layer like yours. Third, the binding constraint is identification, not dollars: at n=300 the study can detect a 3-point effect only between nested policies, so the question and the hypotheses must be sized to the data before the data are collected.

The full question is still the right long-term program, and the pilot's assets (typed failures, a runtime, pre-registration habits) are the right assets for it. But it is publishable as a ladder: accounting and fixed rules first, a strict second label next, and a learned policy only if a pre-registered gate says the data can support one. Resample or Reroute's retreat from v1 to v3 is the best argument for building the ladder in that order.
